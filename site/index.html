The Tetrasemaphore

The Tetrasemaphore in one paragraph

The Tetrasemaphore is four coins that mark how a piece of work stands: NORMATIVE, NON-NORMATIVE, METACOGNITIVE or CRITICAL. Each state is a move of a flat picture, and each coin carries a mark built to survive its own move, written in the symbols chemists and mathematicians already use for symmetry. It is the successor to the Owl Semaphore, which carried the same four states with an owl. An owl means different things in different cultures, so the Tetrasemaphore carries the states with mathematical notation instead. Every claim it makes can be checked with your body, a pen and paper, a phone, a printer, a proof checker or a friend, and the one question still open, whether it helps people think about their thinking, can be tested.

What it is

Carey James Balboa, who started the project, put its purpose this way on 5 September 2026: “We're going to try to teach people to think about their thinking.” The Tetrasemaphore asks two yes-or-no questions of any piece of thinking. Is it looking at its object, or back at its own frame: the premises, method and assumptions it judges from? And does it keep its stance toward the standard, or reverse that stance, the way a mirror reverses left and right? Two yes-or-no questions have exactly four answers. NORMATIVE keeps its stance and looks at its object: what has been tested and holds, for now. NON-NORMATIVE mirrors its stance and looks at its object: exploration that stands alongside the standard without claiming to replace it. METACOGNITIVE keeps its stance and looks at its own frame, which is thinking about thinking. CRITICAL does both at once: it turns over the framework a claim rests on, treating every baseline assumption as possibly wrong. The Tetrasemaphore proposes holding two questions about any piece of thinking as two mirrors at a right angle, so that changing your stance or your focus becomes a move you can see, make and undo. In a flat picture the same four answers are four moves: leave it alone (I), flip it left to right (σᵥ), flip it top to bottom (σₕ), or turn it half way round (C₂), which is both flips at once. NORMATIVE is I, NON-NORMATIVE is σᵥ, METACOGNITIVE is σₕ and CRITICAL is C₂. The moves and their symbols were part of mathematics long before this project, and the coins point at them. As Carey put it (3 October 2026): “That's what we do when we discover math. Honor finally what is.” Each coin's mark is built to survive its own move: the σᵥ mark only the left-right flip, the σₕ mark only the top-bottom flip, the C₂ mark only the half-turn, and the I mark, whose move changes nothing, every move.

The coins are for naming the state of your own thinking, not for labelling anyone else's. That doesn't mean people can't be read: we read each other every day, better than chance for some things and only barely for others, such as lies, and we misread ourselves too. What turns either kind of reading into a measurement is knowing how often it is wrong. This starts with you, and it is meant for everyone.

Because the four answer two questions rather than belonging to one field, they can be laid over systems that already sort their claims: the binding requirements of internet standards (RFC 2119) and their informative notes, peer review's findings and hypotheses, a security advisory's critical flag, a laboratory's calibration audit. The Owl Semaphore grew out of the first of these. They are places to read the four, not the edges of what they are.

Why we left the owl

The project began as the Owl Semaphore, which carried the same four states with the owl of Athena, read in ancient Greece as a sign of wisdom. Each state was the owl itself moved by its operation: left as it was, flipped left to right, flipped top to bottom, or turned half way round.

Translating it for Latin American readers turned that into a question we could check: does an owl mean the same thing everywhere? It does not, and the differences do not fall where we expected. In sixteenth-century central Mexico, Sahagún's Nahua informants said that hearing the horned owl meant “death or sickness; it was an omen of death”. In the K'iche' Maya Popol Vuh, four owls are the messengers of Xibalba, the underworld. Told to sacrifice a young woman and bring back her heart, they spare her, and she tells them they will be esteemed on the face of the earth. In ancient Greece the owl came to stand for wisdom and for Athena, and after 1800 the same bird was seen there as fearsome again; in a recent survey there, most people called owls beneficial, and some still called them bad omens. And when people in three rural communities near Manizales, Colombia, were asked directly, most adults called owls harmless or simply birds, and more called them good luck than bad luck (21 % against 9 %). The readings differ between countries, between centuries, and between neighbours. The sources are listed at the end of this section, so they can be checked.

These are living traditions, not only history. One of our editions for Latin American readers drew on the imagery of the Muisca, a living people of the Colombian Andes. In a mandate that ONIC, Colombia's national indigenous organization, published on 4 May 2024, the authorities of four Muisca resguardos rejected violations of their right to consultation and to free, prior and informed consent, and condemned the use of their knowledge systems and cultural identity for commercial gain, made worse when it is done by people outside their communities. A coin on a shirt that is sold is commercial use. On 17 September 2026, the day we read the mandate, we stopped looking for a carrier in any culture, and the notation itself became the carrier. The dated record of where each edition broke down is in the decision log.

I can't speak for any of these peoples, and neither can the AI systems that helped me build this. So we did what we could check: we read the sources, and we stopped where a living people had spoken for themselves. — Carey James Balboa, signed 11 October 2026

A printed coin cannot know who is looking at it, so when a picture's meaning changes from reader to reader, its maker has no way to correct for it. This project calls the meaning a picture brings with it, beyond the structure it carries, carrier colour. In Carey's words (9 October 2026, revised by him on 21 October): “The whole thing disintegrates, and that is why you realize how powerful carrier color is.” The Tetrasemaphore keeps the four states and their moves and carries them with notation, so the state is read from which move the mark survives, not from what an owl means to the reader. That is the aim of the whole project: a message each reader can check for themselves, which asks no one to take on or give up a belief in order to receive it. Some carrier colour remains, and it can now be listed. The letter σ, the four colours and the look of the coin are choices made here. Which mirror goes with which state is a convention that no mathematics supplies, so each coin states it, with its word and with the gold line that shows its mirror. In the owl, the bird's meaning and the mathematics were one object and could not be pulled apart. The Owl Semaphore's published versions stay as they are, under their own name; the Tetrasemaphore has its own record, linked to them as their successor.

How to test it

Every claim here can be checked by someone who does not trust us. Each check below says what it needs, and the first two need nothing but your body, or a pen and a scrap of paper. With your body: have someone stand in front of you and watch your head. Stand as you are, and nothing changes (I). Turn around: to them, your left and right swap, and your top stays on top (σᵥ). Face them, bend over and look between your legs: your top and bottom swap, and your left and right stay put (σₕ). Turn around, then look between your legs: both swap, and you are looking back at where you started, upside down (C₂). Each move is its own way back, and any two of the last three make the other. The two moves are the two questions, done with the body: turning around reverses the way you face, as NON-NORMATIVE reverses its stance toward the standard, and looking back between your legs turns your view away from what is in front of you and back past yourself, as METACOGNITIVE turns from its object back to its own frame. With a pen and paper: write the letters I, A, D and N in block capitals, hold the page up to a window, and turn it over side to side, then top to bottom, then spin it half way round, each time from the start. I survives every move, A only the side-to-side turn, D only the top-to-bottom turn, and N only the half-turn: the same four as the coins. With a printer or a phone: print the coins or open them on a screen, look only at the mark in the middle, and flip and turn each one. Switch the screen to greyscale, and the four still differ by shape alone. With a phone's formula reader: point it at each of the four camera cards and see whether it reads I, σᵥ, σₕ and C₂. With a computer: the algebra is machine-checked in the Lean proof assistant, with a script that runs under bash, wherever Lean is installed, and a report on what the proofs cover and what they do not: they cover the algebra, not the artwork. With another person: this is the test that matters most. Teach someone the rule, that each mark survives its own move, in five minutes; then show them prints flipped or turned at random, or make the four moves with your body in a random order, and ask which state each one is. Guessing gets one in four right, so if people who have just learned the rule do little better, the system does not teach, whatever the mathematics says. Whether the coins help people think about their thinking is the open question, and these tests are how to find out. The details follow.

How this was made

Carey James Balboa started the project, made every final decision and is responsible for everything published under it. The work was done with AI systems, and nothing here hides that. Carey's work with AI began with OpenAI's ChatGPT. Claude Science (Anthropic) measured and checked every build, wrote the Lean proofs and drafted this note. Carey's signed statement in “Why we left the owl” was drafted with Claude Science from his words of 11 October and approved by him. Claude Code (Anthropic) built the coins and the decision pages, attacked the proofs and ran the red teams. Hermes, an AI agent that switches among models from many companies, kept the shared record and prepared this website. SciSpace, an AI research assistant, worked with Carey from April 2026 across the DNS Tool, the Owl Semaphore and this project, in 18 sessions through September, on research, citation checks and outside review. Among other work, it added citations, retrieved sources, split carrier colour into a perceptual and a social sense, and withdrew two claims. Claude Chat (Anthropic) drafted methods note 01 from a dialogue with Carey. Claude Chat and Google's AI Mode took part in dialogues that the decision log cites. Many other AI models, from most of the major makers, were used on purpose: to look for breaks, and for what held across all of them. Because AI models tend to make the same mistakes as one another (Kim, Garg, Peng & Garg 2025, arXiv:2506.07962), what held across them was then checked against proofs, primary sources and measurements. Where an AI system made a mistake, the failure-modes register says so, and how it was caught.
