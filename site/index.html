The Tetrasemaphore in one paragraph

The Tetrasemaphore is four coins that mark how a piece of work stands: NORMATIVE, NON-NORMATIVE, METACOGNITIVE or CRITICAL. Each state is a move of a flat picture, and each coin carries a mark built to survive its own move, written in the symbols chemists and mathematicians already use for symmetry. Every claim it makes can be checked with your body, a pen and paper, a phone, a printer, a proof checker or a friend, and the one question still open, whether it helps people think about their thinking, can be tested.

The aim of the whole project is to point at some mathematics that was always there, for each reader to check for themselves and think with. It asks no one to take on or give up a belief.

People picked up the abacus, then the calculator. Some abacus users learned to put it down and move the beads in their heads. The four moves are meant to be learned the same way: first with your body or a scrap of paper, then in your mind. Whether that helps is what the tests are for.

What it is

Carey James Balboa, who started the project, put its purpose this way on 5 September 2026: “We're going to try to teach people to think about their thinking.” The Tetrasemaphore asks two yes-or-no questions of any piece of thinking. Is it looking at its object, or back at its own frame: the premises, method and assumptions it judges from? And does it keep its stance toward the standard, or reverse that stance, the way a mirror reverses left and right? Two yes-or-no questions have exactly four answers. NORMATIVE keeps its stance and looks at its object: what has been tested and holds, for now. NON-NORMATIVE mirrors its stance and looks at its object: exploration that stands alongside the standard without claiming to replace it. METACOGNITIVE keeps its stance and looks at its own frame, which is thinking about thinking. CRITICAL does both at once: it turns over the framework a claim rests on, treating every baseline assumption as possibly wrong. The Tetrasemaphore proposes holding two questions about any piece of thinking as two mirrors at a right angle, so that changing your stance or your focus becomes a move you can see, make and undo. In a flat picture the same four answers are four moves: leave it alone (I), flip it left to right (σᵥ), flip it top to bottom (σₕ), or turn it half way round (C₂), which is both flips at once. NORMATIVE is I, NON-NORMATIVE is σᵥ, METACOGNITIVE is σₕ and CRITICAL is C₂. The moves and their symbols were part of mathematics long before this project, and the coins point at them. As Carey put it (3 October 2026): “That's what we do when we discover math. Honor finally what is.” Psychology has met this structure before: Jean Piaget held that formal reasoning uses four transformations that combine exactly as these four moves do (his INRC group; Inhelder & Piaget 1958). Each coin's mark is built to survive its own move: the σᵥ mark only the left-right flip, the σₕ mark only the top-bottom flip, the C₂ mark only the half-turn, and the I mark, whose move changes nothing, every move.

The coins are for naming the state of your own thinking, not for labelling anyone else's. That doesn't mean people can't be read: we read each other every day, better than chance for some things and only barely for others, such as lies, and we misread ourselves too. What turns either kind of reading into a measurement is knowing how often it is wrong. This starts with you, and it is meant for everyone.

How to test it

Every claim here can be checked by someone who does not trust us. Each check below says what it needs, and the first two need nothing but your body, or a pen and a scrap of paper. With your body: have someone stand in front of you and watch your head. Stand as you are, and nothing changes (I). Turn around: to them, your left and right swap, and your top stays on top (σᵥ). Face them, bend over and look between your legs: your top and bottom swap, and your left and right stay put (σₕ). Turn around, then look between your legs: both swap, and you are looking back at where you started, upside down (C₂). Each move is its own way back, and any two of the last three make the other. The two moves are the two questions, done with the body: turning around reverses the way you face, as NON-NORMATIVE reverses its stance toward the standard, and looking back between your legs turns your view away from what is in front of you and back past yourself, as METACOGNITIVE turns from its object back to its own frame. With a pen and paper: write the letters I, A, D and N in block capitals, hold the page up to a window, and turn it over side to side, then top to bottom, then spin it half way round, each time from the start. I survives every move, A only the side-to-side turn, D only the top-to-bottom turn, and N only the half-turn: the same four as the coins. With a printer or a phone: print the coins or open them on a screen, look only at the mark in the middle, and flip and turn each one. Switch the screen to greyscale, and the four still differ by shape alone. With a phone's formula reader: point it at each of the four camera cards and see whether it reads I, σᵥ, σₕ and C₂. With a computer: the algebra is machine-checked in the Lean proof assistant, with a script that runs under bash, wherever Lean is installed, and a report on what the proofs cover and what they do not: they cover the algebra, not the artwork. With another person: this is the test that matters most. Teach someone the rule, that each mark survives its own move, in five minutes; then show them prints flipped or turned at random, or make the four moves with your body in a random order, and ask which state each one is. Guessing gets one in four right, so if people who have just learned the rule do little better, the system does not teach, whatever the mathematics says. Whether the coins help people think about their thinking is the open question, and these tests are how to find out. The details follow.
