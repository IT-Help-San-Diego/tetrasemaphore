The Tetrasemaphore in one paragraph

The Tetrasemaphore is four semaphores that mark how a piece of work stands: NORMATIVE, NON-NORMATIVE, METACOGNITIVE or CRITICAL. Each state is a move of a flat picture, and each semaphore carries a mark built to survive its own move, written in the symbols chemists and mathematicians already use for symmetry. Every claim it makes can be checked with your body, a pen and paper, a phone, a printer, a proof checker or a friend, and the one question still open, whether it helps people think about their thinking, can be tested.

What it is

The Tetrasemaphore is an attempt to teach people to think about their thinking. It asks two yes-or-no questions of any piece of thinking. Is it looking at its object, or back at its own frame: the premises, method and assumptions it judges from? And does it keep its stance toward the standard, or reverse that stance, the way a mirror reverses left and right? Two yes-or-no questions have exactly four answers. NORMATIVE keeps its stance and looks at its object: what has been tested and holds, for now. NON-NORMATIVE mirrors its stance and looks at its object: exploration that stands alongside the standard without claiming to replace it. METACOGNITIVE keeps its stance but turns its look back to its own frame, the way you can keep your feet planted and look back between your legs; this is thinking about thinking. CRITICAL does both at once: it turns over the framework a claim rests on, treating every baseline assumption as possibly wrong. The Tetrasemaphore proposes holding two questions about any piece of thinking as two mirrors at a right angle, so that changing your stance or your focus becomes a move you can see, make and undo. In a flat picture the same four answers are four moves: leave it alone (I), flip it left to right (σᵥ), flip it top to bottom (σₕ), or turn it half way round (C₂), which is both flips at once. NORMATIVE is I, NON-NORMATIVE is σᵥ, METACOGNITIVE is σₕ and CRITICAL is C₂. The moves and their symbols were part of mathematics long before this project, and the semaphores point at them. That's what we do when we discover math: honor finally what is. Psychology has met this structure before: Jean Piaget held that formal reasoning uses four transformations that combine exactly as these four moves do (his INRC group; Inhelder & Piaget 1958). Each semaphore's mark is built to survive its own move: the σᵥ mark only the left-right flip, the σₕ mark only the top-bottom flip, the C₂ mark only the half-turn, and the I mark, whose move changes nothing, every move.

The semaphores are for naming the state of your own thinking, not for labelling anyone else's. That doesn't mean people can't be read: we read each other every day, better than chance for some things and only barely for others, such as lies, and we misread ourselves too. What turns either kind of reading into a measurement is knowing how often it is wrong. This starts with you, and it is meant for everyone.

Because the four answer two questions rather than belonging to one field, they can be laid over systems that already sort their claims: the binding requirements of internet standards (RFC 2119) and their informative notes, peer review's findings and hypotheses, a security advisory's critical flag, a laboratory's calibration audit. The Owl Semaphore grew out of the first of these. They are places to read the four, not the edges of what they are.

How to test it

Every claim here can be checked by someone who does not trust us. Each check below says what it needs, and the first two need nothing but your body, or a pen and a scrap of paper. If turning or bending is hard, the pen-and-paper check makes the same moves. With your body: have someone stand in front of you and watch your head. Stand as you are, and nothing changes (I). Turn around: to them, your left and right swap, and your top stays on top (σᵥ). Face them, bend over and look between your legs: your top and bottom swap, and your left and right stay put (σₕ). A handstand is the same move. Turn around, then look between your legs: both swap, and you are looking back at where you started, upside down (C₂). Each move is its own way back, and any two of the last three make the other. The two moves are the two questions, done with the body: turning around reverses the way you face, as NON-NORMATIVE reverses its stance toward the standard, and looking back between your legs turns your view away from what is in front of you and back past yourself, as METACOGNITIVE turns from its object back to its own frame. With a pen and paper: write the letters I, A, D and N in block capitals, hold the page up to a window, and turn it over side to side, then top to bottom, then spin it half way round, each time from the start. I survives every move, A only the side-to-side turn, D only the top-to-bottom turn, and N only the half-turn: the same four as the semaphores. With a printer or a phone: print the semaphores or open them on a screen, look only at the mark in the middle, and flip and turn each one. Switch the screen to greyscale, and the four still differ by shape alone. With a computer: the algebra is machine-checked in the Lean proof assistant, with a script that runs under bash, wherever Lean is installed, and a report on what the proofs cover and what they do not: they cover the algebra, not the artwork. With another person: this is the test that matters most. Teach someone the rule, that each mark survives its own move, in five minutes; then show them prints flipped or turned at random, or make the four moves with your body in a random order, and ask which state each one is. Guessing gets one in four right, so if people who have just learned the rule do little better, the system does not teach, whatever the mathematics says. Whether the semaphores help people think about their thinking is the open question, and these tests are how to find out. The details follow.

What the tests are for. The moves are made with the body on purpose. The working hypothesis, untested, is that a move learned together with one of the four states can later serve as a cue to stop and check, a way out of a line of thinking, and that with practice it can shrink to a small gesture or a thought. Try the four your own way first: people learn differently, and some may find uses no one here has thought of. Testing it properly needs people who have not read this note, their consent and an ethics review, and it needs funding this project does not have. The test register sets out how to run it, and what is known for and against the idea, for anyone who can.

Reading the semaphores

The symbols. I is the identity, the move that changes nothing. Chemists write it E, and keep i for inversion, which sends every point through the centre to the opposite side; on a flat picture that is the same move as C₂. We write I because the identity's mark has to look the same after every move, and I is the only one of the usual identity symbols (I, E, 1, e) whose letter does. σ is a reflection and C₂ a half-turn, in chemists' symbols. The small letter after σ names the mirror line on the page: v for vertical, h for horizontal. Chemists measure v and h from a molecule's main turning axis instead. On a flat picture that axis points straight out of the page, so in their notation both of these mirrors count as vertical (σv and σv′), and their σh would be a reflection through the picture itself, which moves nothing you can see. The gold line on each semaphore shows which line we mean.

Check the mark, not the ring. The ring's keys all turn one way, so a mirror reverses them, while a half-turn leaves the ring as it was. With its words, every whole semaphore changes under σᵥ, σₕ and C₂. Only the mark can survive a move: NORMATIVE's survives all three, and each of the others survives its own.

The gold. Gold shows what the semaphore's move leaves in place: the whole figure for I, a vertical line for σᵥ, a horizontal line for σₕ, a single point for C₂. That is also how to tell I from inversion: inversion leaves only the centre in place, so its gold would be a single point. Lay the two σ semaphores over each other and their lines cross exactly at C₂'s point. Doing one mirror and then the other is the half-turn, and the points both mirrors leave alone are the point the half-turn leaves alone.

Colour comes second. Shape carries every state. The two σ colours are equally light, so it is their axis, not their brightness, that tells them apart, which is all the mathematics says separates them.

The tests in detail

Every claim above can be checked by someone who does not trust us. The body and paper checks above come first. The tests run from the cheapest to the most demanding.

  1. Do the moves. Print the four semaphores, or open them on a phone, and look only at the mark in the middle. Flip each semaphore left to right, flip it top to bottom, and turn it half way round. The σᵥ mark comes back unchanged only after the left-right flip, the σₕ mark only after the top-bottom flip, and the C₂ mark only after the half-turn. The I mark comes back unchanged after all three. Any other mark that survives a move that is not its own is a mistake in the design.
  2. Two mirrors make a half-turn. Write an F on tracing paper. Flip it left to right, then top to bottom: it lands exactly where a half-turn puts it. Then lay the two σ semaphores over each other, printed on transparencies or at half opacity on a screen. Their gold lines cross at the point that C₂'s gold marks.
  3. Take the colour away. Switch your phone to greyscale, or photocopy the semaphores in black and white. All four can still be told apart by shape. The two σ semaphores are equally light, so in grey it is their axis that tells them apart, which is the only thing the mathematics says separates them.
  4. Check the proofs. The algebra is machine-checked in Lean. Install the Lean toolchain, run ./check.sh under bash in the proofs/ folder, and read the axiom report it prints: it lists everything each theorem assumes. Then try to break it: change a definition and watch the check fail. The proofs cover the algebra, not the artwork.
  5. Teach it to someone. This is the test that matters most. Explain the three moves and the rule, that each mark survives its own move, to someone in five minutes. Cover the words, then show them twenty prints, each one flipped, turned half way round, or left alone at random, or make the four moves with your body in a random order, and ask which state each one is. None of the four moves changes which move a mark survives, so the state can always be read. A quarter turn is different: it carries one mirror onto the other, so a print turned a quarter of the way round reads as the other σ semaphore. That is why the two σ semaphores are twins. Guessing gets one in four right. With twenty tries, nine or more right would come from guessing less than one time in twenty. If people who have just learned the rule do little better than one in four, the system does not teach, whatever the mathematics says.
  6. Ask what the picture means. Show an owl semaphore and a notation semaphore to people from different places, and write down what each person says the semaphore means. The prediction is that answers to the owl vary with where people grew up, and answers to the notation depend only on whether they have learned it. If the notation's answers vary as much as the owl's, that part of the theory is wrong.

Challenges to put to it

  • Find a σ or C₂ mark that survives a move that is not its own. Test 1 is how to look.
  • Tell the two σ semaphores apart without their gold lines or their colours. Only the direction of the mirror should do it. Any other difference carries something the mathematics does not.
  • Add a fifth state. The four moves are two yes-or-no choices: flip left to right or not, and flip top to bottom or not. Two choices make exactly four combinations: neither (I), the first (σᵥ), the second (σₕ), both (C₂). A fifth state would need a third choice, and three choices make eight states, not five.
  • Is a bar of four beats the same four? No. Move ahead one beat, twice, and you land on beat three, not back where you began; getting back takes four steps. On the semaphores, any move made twice brings you back. Same number, different structure: mathematicians call the first the cyclic group of order 4, and the second the Klein four-group.
  • Find a theorem counted as content that only restates its own definitions. The red-team report (proofs/RED_TEAM_LEAN.md, section 1) sorts every theorem by kind; check it against the file.

Why we left the owl

The project began as the Owl Semaphore, which carried the same four states with the owl of Athena, read in ancient Greece as a sign of wisdom. Each state was the owl itself moved by its operation: left as it was, flipped left to right, flipped top to bottom, or turned half way round.

Translating it for Latin American readers turned that into a question we could check: does an owl mean the same thing everywhere? It does not, and the differences do not fall where we expected. In sixteenth-century central Mexico, Sahagún's Nahua informants said that hearing the horned owl meant “death or sickness; it was an omen of death”. In the K'iche' Maya Popol Vuh, four owls are the messengers of Xibalba, the underworld. Told to sacrifice a young woman and bring back her heart, they spare her, and she tells them they will be esteemed on the face of the earth. In ancient Greece the owl came to stand for wisdom and for Athena, and after 1800 the same bird was seen there as fearsome again; in a recent survey there, most people called owls beneficial, and some still called them bad omens. And when people in three rural communities near Manizales, Colombia, were asked directly, most adults called owls harmless or simply birds, and more called them good luck than bad luck (21 % against 9 %). The readings differ between countries, between centuries, and between neighbours. The sources are listed at the end of this section, so they can be checked.

These are living traditions, not only history. One of our editions for Latin American readers drew on the imagery of the Muisca, a living people of the Colombian Andes. In a mandate that ONIC, Colombia's national indigenous organization, published on 4 May 2024, the authorities of four Muisca resguardos rejected violations of their right to consultation and to free, prior and informed consent, and condemned the use of their knowledge systems and cultural identity for commercial gain, made worse when it is done by people outside their communities. A semaphore on a shirt that is sold is commercial use. On 17 September 2026, the day we read the mandate, we stopped looking for a carrier in any culture, and the notation itself became the carrier. The dated record of where each edition broke down is in the decision log.

I can't speak for any of these peoples, and neither can the AI systems that helped me build this. So we did what we could check: we read the sources, and we stopped where a living people had spoken for themselves. — Carey James Balboa, signed 11 October 2026

A printed semaphore cannot know who is looking at it, so when a picture's meaning changes from reader to reader, its maker has no way to correct for it. This project calls the meaning a picture brings with it, beyond the structure it carries, carrier colour. With the owl, the whole thing disintegrates, and that is why you realize how powerful carrier colour is. The Tetrasemaphore keeps the four states and their moves and carries them with notation, so the state is read from which move the mark survives, not from what an owl means to the reader. That is the aim of the whole project: to point at some mathematics that was always there, for each reader to check for themselves and think with. It asks no one to take on or give up a belief. Some carrier colour remains, and it can now be listed. The letter σ, the four colours and the look of the semaphores are choices made here. Which mirror goes with which state is a convention that no mathematics supplies, so each semaphore states it, with its word and with the gold line that shows its mirror. In the owl, the bird's meaning and the mathematics were one object and could not be pulled apart. The Owl Semaphore's published versions stay as they are, under their own name; the Tetrasemaphore has its own record, linked to them as their successor.

How this was made

Many other AI models, from most of the major makers, were used on purpose: to look for breaks, and for what held across all of them. Because AI models tend to make the same mistakes as one another (Kim, Garg, Peng & Garg 2025, arXiv:2506.07962), what held across them was then checked against proofs, primary sources and measurements. Where an AI system made a mistake, the failure-modes register says so, and how it was caught.
