Method & tests

Reading the coins

The symbols. I is the identity, the move that changes nothing. Chemists write it E, and keep i for inversion, which sends every point through the centre to the opposite side; on a flat picture that is the same move as C₂. We write I because the identity's mark has to look the same after every move, and I is the only one of the usual identity symbols (I, E, 1, e) whose letter does. σ is a reflection and C₂ a half-turn, in chemists' symbols. The small letter after σ names the mirror line on the page: v for vertical, h for horizontal. Chemists measure v and h from a molecule's main turning axis instead. On a flat picture that axis points straight out of the page, so in their notation both of these mirrors count as vertical (σv and σv′), and their σh would be a reflection through the picture itself, which moves nothing you can see. The gold line on each coin shows which line we mean.

Check the mark, not the ring. The ring's keys all turn one way, so a mirror reverses them, while a half-turn leaves the ring as it was. With its words, every whole coin changes under every move; only the mark survives its own.

The gold. Gold shows what the coin's move leaves in place: the whole figure for I, a vertical line for σᵥ, a horizontal line for σₕ, a single point for C₂. That is also how to tell I from inversion: inversion leaves only the centre in place, so its gold would be a single point. Lay the two σ coins over each other and their lines cross exactly at C₂'s point. Doing one mirror and then the other is the half-turn, and the points both mirrors leave alone are the point the half-turn leaves alone.

Colour comes second. Shape carries every state. In greyscale the two σ coins are the same grey, and only their axis tells them apart, which is all the mathematics says separates them.

The tests in detail

Every claim on the home page can be checked by someone who does not trust us. The body and paper checks on the home page come first. The tests run from the cheapest to the most demanding.

  1. Do the moves. Print the four coins, or open them on a phone, and look only at the mark in the middle. Flip each coin left to right, flip it top to bottom, and turn it half way round. The σᵥ mark comes back unchanged only after the left-right flip, the σₕ mark only after the top-bottom flip, and the C₂ mark only after the half-turn. The I mark comes back unchanged after all three. A mark that survives a move that is not its own is a mistake in the design.
  2. Two mirrors make a half-turn. Write an F on tracing paper. Flip it left to right, then top to bottom: it lands exactly where a half-turn puts it. Then lay the two σ coins over each other, printed on transparencies or at half opacity on a screen. Their gold lines cross at the point that C₂'s gold marks.
  3. Take the colour away. Switch your phone to greyscale, or photocopy the coins in black and white. All four can still be told apart by shape. The two σ coins differ only in the direction of their gold line, which is the only thing the mathematics says separates them.
  4. Ask a camera. Point a formula reader, such as Google Lens or a phone camera's text recognition, at each of the four camera cards, one at a time. A pass is I, σᵥ, σₕ and C₂. "It found a formula" is not a pass: a horizontal line between two symbols reads as a fraction bar. Our result (Carey, October 2026): the phone readers he tried did not read the cards reliably. Reading formulas from pictures is still a young technology, so this test is worth repeating as it improves.
  5. Check the proofs. The algebra is machine-checked in Lean. Install the Lean toolchain, run ./check.sh under bash, and read the axiom report it prints: it lists everything each theorem assumes. Then try to break it: change a definition and watch a theorem fail. The proofs cover the algebra, not the artwork.
  6. Teach it to someone. This is the test that matters most. Explain the three moves and the rule, that each mark survives its own move, to someone in five minutes. Cover the words, then show them twenty prints, each one flipped, turned half way round, or left alone at random, or make the four moves with your body in a random order, and ask which state each one is. None of the four moves changes which move a mark survives, so the state can always be read. A quarter turn is different: it carries one mirror onto the other, so a print turned a quarter of the way round reads as the other σ coin. That is why the two σ coins are twins. Guessing gets one in four right. If people who have just learned the rule do little better than one in four, the system does not teach, whatever the mathematics says.
  7. Ask what the picture means. Show an owl coin and a notation coin to people from different places, and write down what each person says the coin means. The prediction is that answers to the owl vary with where people grew up, and answers to the notation depend only on whether they have learned it. If the notation's answers vary as much as the owl's, that part of the theory is wrong.

Challenges to put to it

  • Find a mark that survives a move that is not its own. Test 1 is how to look.
  • Tell the two σ coins apart without their gold lines. Only the direction of the mirror should do it. Any other difference carries something the mathematics does not.
  • Add a fifth state. The four moves are two yes-or-no choices: flip left to right or not, and flip top to bottom or not. Two choices make exactly four combinations: neither (I), the first (σᵥ), the second (σₕ), both (C₂). A fifth state would need a third choice, and three choices make eight states, not five.
  • Find a theorem in the Lean file that only restates its own definitions. The red-team report lists which theorems are bookkeeping and which carry content; check it against the file.
